EU Court Rules AI-Generated Art Can Infringe Copyright

A Munich court has ruled that storing copyrighted lyrics in AI model parameters constitutes an infringement, marking a landmark judicial extension of EU copyright law to synthetic media training.

Editor’s brief

Why this matters now

This article examines a pivotal legal development in the European Union regarding artificial intelligence and intellectual property. It analyzes recent court rulings, specifically from Munich, which have begun to define the boundaries of copyright infringement in the context of AI model training and output. The evidence is drawn from legal analyses of these specific judgments, providing a snapshot of how existing EU copyright frameworks are being applied to new technologies by the judiciary rather than through new legislation.

Assignment angle: Focuses on a recent landmark judicial decision extending copyright protection to human creators against AI training use, distinct from legislative proposals.

FIELD NOTE

The Munich Ruling: Parameters as Fixation

A Munich court has issued a significant decision in the case of GEMA v. OpenAI, holding that AI models can infringe copyright by embedding copyrighted works without a license. The court's reasoning centered on the legal concept of 'fixation' under EU copyright law. It determined that the storage of copyrighted lyrics within the model's parameters satisfied this requirement. Consequently, the subsequent reproduction of these lyrics in the AI's outputs was deemed an infringement. This ruling provides a judicial basis for claiming that the training process itself, not just the final output, can be subject to copyright law.

  • Munich Regional Court ruled in favor of GEMA against OpenAI.
  • Storage of lyrics in model parameters was found to satisfy the fixation requirement.
  • Reproduction of lyrics in AI outputs was deemed a copyright infringement.

Transatlantic Reach and Extraterritoriality

Building on the initial ruling, a second decision by the Munich District Court on July 31, 2026, expanded the scope of this legal precedent. The court held that training an AI model on protected musical works without a license can infringe copyright even when the training process itself occurs outside the European Union. This extraterritorial application is a notable development, suggesting that AI companies operating globally may need to secure licenses for EU-protected works regardless of where their servers are located. This decision is likely to influence how international AI firms structure their training data acquisition.

  • Second Munich District Court ruling on July 31, 2026, addressed extraterritoriality.
  • Training on protected works without a license can infringe copyright even if done outside the EU.
  • The ruling has implications for global AI companies' data practices.

Contrasting Rulings on AI-Generated Output

While the Munich rulings focus on the training process, other German courts have addressed the copyrightability of the AI-generated works themselves. In a separate case, the Munich Local Court dismissed a lawsuit seeking an injunction against three AI-generated logos. This decision aligns with a broader trend in case law that denies copyright protection to works generated entirely by AI without sufficient human creative input. This creates a two-part legal landscape: the training data may be protected from unauthorized use, but the resulting AI-generated content may not be eligible for its own copyright protection.

  • Munich Local Court dismissed a copyright claim for AI-generated logos.
  • This ruling joins a majority of case law denying protection to purely AI-generated works.
  • A distinction is emerging between the protection of training data and the copyrightability of AI output.

FIELD NOTE

The Human Creativity Requirement

The underlying principle in these rulings on AI-generated output is the persistent requirement for human creativity. The European Parliament's Think Tank has noted that while the EU lacks specific rules on the copyrightability of AI-generated works, existing case law from the Court of Justice of the European Union and developments at the Member State level demonstrate a strong need for human creativity to establish copyright. This principle is what led to the dismissal of the claim for the AI-generated logos. For a work to be protected, there must be evidence of the author's own intellectual creation, a standard that purely algorithmic generation may not meet.

  • EU case law emphasizes the need for human creativity for copyright protection.
  • The European Parliament Think Tank confirms this is a strong trend across member states.
  • This principle explains why AI-generated logos were not granted copyright.

Closing read

Conclusion

The recent Munich court rulings represent a significant judicial step in defining the relationship between AI and copyright in Europe. By ruling that the storage of copyrighted works in AI model parameters constitutes an infringement, the courts have provided a legal tool for rights holders to challenge the use of their works in AI training. The subsequent ruling on extraterritoriality further extends this protection, potentially impacting AI developers worldwide. However, this protection for training data exists alongside a parallel trend of denying copyright to the AI-generated outputs themselves, based on the lack of human creative input. This creates a complex legal environment where the inputs are protected but the outputs may not be.

Five takeaways

  • A Munich court has ruled that storing copyrighted lyrics in AI model parameters is a copyright infringement.
  • A subsequent Munich ruling extended this protection to training that occurs outside the EU.
  • Other German courts have ruled that AI-generated works, like logos, are not eligible for copyright without human creative input.
  • The requirement for human creativity remains a central principle in EU copyright law as applied to AI.
  • The legal landscape is evolving rapidly, with judicial decisions playing a key role in defining the rules for AI and intellectual property.
