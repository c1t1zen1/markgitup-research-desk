Real-Time Behavioral Biometrics: AI Detecting Synthetic Identity Fraud in Digital Onboarding

As synthetic identities challenge one-time credential checks, behavioral biometrics could give digital onboarding a richer fraud signal—while introducing privacy, biometric-theft, and governance risks.

Editor’s brief

Why this matters now

Synthetic identity fraud is described in the supplied evidence as an identity assembled from fabricated or manipulated information rather than simply stolen from one person. The assignment’s distinct angle is the move from static credential checks toward dynamic analysis of how applicants behave during digital onboarding. Okta defines behavioral biometrics as analysis of behaviors and physical activities to help prevent and identify fraud, while Deloitte identifies biometrics and behavioral cues as potential defenses against synthetic identity fraud.

This article synthesizes the supplied excerpts from Fraud.net, Plaid, Deloitte, Okta, Entrust, Aware, and BIIA. The evidence is uneven: several sources provide only short summaries, and BIIA explicitly presents its 2026 claims as predictions. No statistics, performance comparisons, or independent validation are added beyond the supplied material.

Assignment angle: This focuses on the shift from static credential checks to dynamic behavioral analysis to combat sophisticated synthetic identity fraud emerging in digital onboarding processes.

FIELD NOTE

Synthetic identities expose the limits of one-time checks

Fraud.net presents synthetic identity theft as an identity that can be "conjured up out of thin air," while Plaid describes synthetic identity fraud as a sophisticated form of fraud requiring detection and prevention strategies. These descriptions establish the central challenge: an applicant may present an identity package that does not map cleanly to one conventional stolen identity.

For digital onboarding, that makes the interaction itself relevant to the fraud decision. A submitted credential or document can remain only one part of the assessment; the assignment’s central proposition is that dynamic behavioral evidence may reveal additional anomalies that static identity checks do not capture. The supplied sources do not quantify how often this occurs or prove that behavioral analysis outperforms existing checks.

  • Fraud.net describes synthetic identity theft as an identity created rather than simply taken from a single victim.
  • Plaid frames synthetic identity fraud as sophisticated and requiring dedicated detection and prevention strategies.
  • The shift toward behavioral analysis is an analytical direction in this article, not a measured performance claim from the supplied excerpts.

Behavior becomes an additional identity signal

Okta says behavioral biometrics analyze behaviors and physical activities to help prevent and identify fraud. That definition broadens identity assessment beyond what an applicant submits: the system can consider interaction patterns as another category of evidence during an identity-related event.

Deloitte’s excerpt similarly says biometrics and behavioral cues can be powerful tools against synthetic identity fraud, while warning that generative AI may also intensify identity fraud. The evidence therefore supports behavioral biometrics as a potentially useful signal, not as a standalone identity proof or guaranteed decision mechanism.

  • Behavioral biometrics focus on behavior and physical activity rather than only static identity attributes.
  • Deloitte identifies behavioral cues and biometrics as potential fraud-fighting tools.
  • Behavioral analysis should be treated as an additional signal because the supplied evidence does not establish that it can replace documents or other identity controls.

FIELD NOTE

Layered AI defense matters more than a single model

Entrust’s excerpt describes the future of fraud prevention as identity-centric and AI-driven, with protection extending across people, documents, biometrics, and systems. This supports a layered architecture in which behavioral biometrics contribute to a broader identity defense rather than carrying the entire decision.

The layered approach also reflects the changing threat environment. Entrust says fraudsters are adopting new tools and tactics, and Deloitte warns that generative AI may supercharge identity fraud even as AI provides defensive capabilities. The practical implication is a continuing contest between automated fraud creation and automated fraud detection, although the excerpts do not provide comparative evidence about which side currently has an advantage.

  • Entrust advocates coverage across people, documents, biometrics, and systems.
  • AI can support fraud prevention, but Deloitte also identifies generative AI as a possible accelerator of identity fraud.
  • A behavioral signal is more defensible as one layer in an identity-centric system than as a replacement for all other checks.

FIELD NOTE

Privacy and biometric theft remain part of the threat model

Aware’s source description places AI fraud and identity trends alongside privacy, authentication, and digital trust. That framing matters because behavioral biometric systems analyze highly sensitive interaction or physical-activity signals. The supplied excerpt does not specify a regulatory model, retention period, consent process, or accuracy threshold, so those operational questions remain open.

BIIA’s 2026 predictions identify stolen biometric data as a possible new vector for synthetic identity creation and connect synthetic identity fraud with broader AI-driven cybercrime trends. This is explicitly a forecast, not an established result in the supplied evidence. It reinforces the need to evaluate behavioral biometrics as both a defensive capability and a potential target.

  • Privacy and authentication are named as central themes in Aware’s discussion of biometric security trends.
  • BIIA forecasts that stolen biometric data could become a vector for synthetic identity creation.
  • Entrust’s layered model implies that biometric protection must sit alongside document, system, and identity controls.

Closing read

Conclusion

The supplied evidence supports a cautious case for real-time behavioral biometrics in digital onboarding. Okta provides the clearest definition of the technology, Deloitte connects behavioral cues and biometrics with synthetic identity defense, and Entrust argues for an identity-centric system spanning multiple layers. Together, these sources support treating behavior as an additional fraud signal rather than assuming that one check can establish identity.

The forward-looking case is less certain. Deloitte warns that generative AI may strengthen identity fraud, while BIIA forecasts stolen biometric data as a possible synthetic-identity vector. Those are projections, not demonstrated outcomes in the supplied material. The likely strategic direction is therefore layered, AI-assisted identity defense with explicit attention to privacy, biometric protection, and the limits of behavioral evidence.
