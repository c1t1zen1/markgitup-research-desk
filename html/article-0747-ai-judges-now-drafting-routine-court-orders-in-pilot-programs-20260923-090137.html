AI Judges Now Drafting Routine Court Orders in Pilot Programs

Pilot programs in California and elsewhere are testing AI tools that help judges draft routine orders and rulings, marking a shift from legal research assistance to direct judicial document generation.

September 23, 2026 · 9:01 AM PDT · 5 min read · 12 sources · Original family: AI in Legal Systems & Law Practice
AIlegal systemscourtsautomation
Editor’s brief

Why this matters now

Artificial intelligence is moving beyond legal research tools into the courtroom itself, with pilot programs now deploying AI systems that help judges draft rulings and prepare for hearings. The most prominent example is the Los Angeles County Superior Court's partnership with Learned Hand, a legal AI startup, announced in March 2026. This development represents a significant evolution in how courts handle their caseloads, as AI transitions from supporting attorneys to assisting judicial officers directly in their decision-making processes.

Assignment angle: Focuses on AI moving beyond legal research to actually generating judicial documents in live pilot courts, a new stage in AI's role within legal systems.

FIELD NOTE

The Los Angeles Pilot Program

The Los Angeles County Superior Court announced a pilot program in March 2026 with Learned Hand, a legal startup that develops AI tools specifically for judges. The program is being tested in some civil courtrooms, where the AI technology assists judges in conducting legal research and drafting rulings. According to court officials, AI-assisted judicial orders will undergo independent review before being published, providing a quality control mechanism for the technology's output. The pilot represents one of the most comprehensive tests of AI in judicial work to date, focusing on high-volume civil cases where routine orders and scheduling decisions consume significant judicial time.

  • Pilot announced in March 2026 with Learned Hand AI startup
  • Focused on civil courtrooms in Los Angeles County
  • AI-assisted orders undergo independent review before publication
  • Designed to help with legal research and ruling drafting

AI Tool Capabilities and Judicial Workflow

The AI tools being tested in these pilot programs can rapidly distill hundreds of pages of legal motions and filings, a task that traditionally consumes considerable judicial time. The technology uses samples of a particular jurist's writing style to help reach conclusions and draft tentative rulings that match the judge's voice and analytical approach. Federal Judge Xavier Rodriguez of Texas, who uses AI in his court, describes feeding relevant court filings into an AI tool that quickly produces a timeline of the case and the claims involved. This capability allows judges to focus more on complex legal questions and less on the mechanical aspects of case preparation and document drafting.

  • Can process hundreds of pages of legal motions rapidly
  • Uses judge's writing style samples for drafting
  • Produces case timelines and claim summaries
  • Helps draft tentative rulings for judicial review

Broader Adoption Across Multiple States

The Los Angeles pilot is not an isolated experiment. According to the CEO of Learned Hand, the company's AI tool is already in use by judges in 10 states, and the tool has also been adopted by the Michigan Supreme Court. This broader adoption suggests that AI assistance for judicial work is becoming an increasingly common practice across the American court system. The spread of these tools indicates growing confidence among judicial officers and court administrators in the technology's ability to enhance efficiency without compromising the quality of judicial decision-making. The multi-state presence also provides opportunities for comparing outcomes and identifying best practices for AI integration in courtrooms.

  • Learned Hand AI used in 10 states according to company CEO
  • Michigan Supreme Court has adopted the tool
  • Growing adoption suggests increasing judicial confidence
  • Multi-state use enables comparison of outcomes

FIELD NOTE

Transparency and Disclosure Requirements

As AI becomes more prevalent in judicial work, questions about transparency and disclosure are emerging. In January 2026, Florida's two largest judicial circuits issued sweeping AI disclosure orders. The Eleventh Judicial Circuit (Miami-Dade County) and the Seventeenth Judicial Circuit (Broward County) now require that any attorney or self-represented litigant who uses AI in preparing court documents must disclose that fact. While these disclosure requirements focus on attorney use rather than judicial use, they reflect a broader trend toward greater transparency about AI's role in the legal process. The California pilot program's commitment to independent review of AI-assisted orders represents another approach to maintaining transparency and accountability in the use of these tools.

  • Florida circuits issued AI disclosure orders in January 2026
  • Attorneys must disclose AI use in document preparation
  • California pilot includes independent review requirement
  • Transparency concerns growing as AI adoption increases

Closing read

Conclusion

The evidence indicates that AI is transitioning from a research tool for legal professionals to an active participant in judicial decision-making processes. The pilot programs, particularly the Los Angeles County Superior Court's partnership with Learned Hand, represent a deliberate and structured approach to testing these capabilities in real-world court environments. The fact that AI-assisted orders will undergo independent review before publication suggests that court officials are taking a cautious, measured approach to this new technology. The broader adoption across 10 states and by the Michigan Supreme Court indicates that this is not merely an experimental curiosity but a growing trend in American jurisprudence.
