AI Models Combat Synthetic Identity Fraud Using Behavioral Biometrics in KYC

Banks are pairing behavioral biometrics, device intelligence, and AI-agent detection with KYC defenses as synthetic identities and automated scams pressure conventional verification.

November 14, 2026 · 3:05 PM PST · 5 min read · 12 sources · Original family: AI Fraud Detection in Banking & Finance
KYCSynthetic IdentityBehavioral BiometricsFraud Detection
Editor’s brief

Why this matters now

Synthetic identity fraud is presented in the supplied evidence as a growing banking risk, alongside account takeovers, AI-driven scams, deepfakes, and fabricated identities. One source cites $2.4 billion in fraud exposure for US lenders, while another says synthetic identity fraud represents 11% of reported fraud; these figures come from different sources and are not directly reconciled.

The evidence points toward layered, proactive defenses rather than document checks alone. Feedzai’s described approach combines AI-agent detection, behavioral biometrics, and device intelligence to identify suspicious account activity. Other sources discuss layered fraud controls, voice biometrics, and the limits of static credentials, but the supplied material does not provide measured performance results for behavioral biometrics specifically.

Assignment angle: This angle focuses on the proactive defense mechanism of AI analyzing subtle user behavior patterns to detect synthetic identities, moving beyond simple document verification.

Synthetic identity fraud expands the KYC problem

Synthetic identity fraud combines the broader challenge of fabricated or manipulated identities with the difficulty of recognizing suspicious activity that may not be obvious during an initial verification step. The supplied sources connect this threat with AI-driven scams, account takeovers, deepfakes, and voice cloning.

Because the sources describe different measures, populations, and contexts, neither figure should be treated as a universal benchmark.

FIELD NOTE

AI changes both defense and attack

The evidence describes an accelerating contest. Banks and fraud teams are evaluating AI-enabled detection, while scam operations and fabricated identities also use AI, malware, automation, deepfakes, and voice cloning. Feedzai’s AI-agent detection is therefore relevant to activity that may be automated or generated by non-human actors.

Market projections indicate expanding commercial interest, not guaranteed effectiveness. MarketsandMarkets projects the BFSI fraud-detection and prevention market to grow from $7.78 billion in 2026 to $15.06 billion by 2031, while a separate projection puts the deepfake-detection market at $12.14 billion by 2035. These are forecasts and should not be read as proof that deployed systems will reduce fraud.

  • Defenders must account for AI-enabled automation as well as conventional fraud.
  • Commercial-market growth signals investment interest, not validated security outcomes.
  • Detection systems may need continuous adaptation as attacker techniques change.

Closing read

Conclusion

The supplied evidence supports a cautious conclusion: behavioral biometrics can strengthen KYC and fraud controls by adding activity-based signals to documents, credentials, device data, and other layers. The clearest source describes behavioral biometrics as part of a digital-trust system for identifying suspicious account activity, while other sources support the broader case for moving beyond static authentication.

The evidence does not prove that behavioral biometrics independently detects synthetic identities, nor does it provide comparative rates for detection, false positives, customer friction, or fraud losses avoided. Market-size figures are forecasts, and the reported fraud statistics use different contexts. The defensible outlook is therefore conditional: layered AI defenses may improve proactive detection, but effectiveness must be demonstrated through institution-specific results and ongoing monitoring.
