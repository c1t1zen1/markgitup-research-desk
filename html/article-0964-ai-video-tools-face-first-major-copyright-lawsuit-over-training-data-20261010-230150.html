AI Video Tools Face First Major Copyright Lawsuit Over Training Data

A wave of copyright lawsuits targeting AI video tools over their training data is reshaping how companies build and deploy generative models, with early rulings signaling the end of an era of unchecked data scraping.

Editor’s brief

Why this matters now

The legal landscape for AI video tools is shifting as courts begin to weigh in on whether the use of copyrighted material in training datasets constitutes infringement. This issue, distinct from debates over deepfake disclosure or ad regulation, strikes at the heart of how generative AI is developed. The evidence gathered from multiple sources indicates a growing number of lawsuits and a clear trend toward greater legal scrutiny of AI training practices.

The Rise of AI Copyright Litigation

Between 2023 and 2024, over 50 copyright lawsuits were filed against AI companies, with the number continuing to grow. By early 2026, the results of these cases are beginning to emerge, signaling a significant change in the legal environment for AI developers. The era of 'train first, ask later' appears to be coming to an end, as courts, regulators, and rights holders increasingly challenge the assumption that publicly accessible content can be freely used for training AI models.

The Core Legal Question

The fundamental legal question in these cases is whether copying entire works—books, news articles, photographs, code repositories, recorded music—into training datasets without a license constitutes direct infringement under copyright law. The claim is that this copying violates the exclusive rights granted to copyright holders, regardless of whether the AI's output is substantially similar to the original works.

  • Core claim: unauthorized copying of works into training datasets
  • Legal basis: direct infringement under 17 U.S.C. § 106
  • Focus on input data, not just output similarity

Impact on AI Video Tools

AI video tools like Sora and Runway are particularly vulnerable to these lawsuits because they rely on large datasets of video content for training. The legal battles over training data could force these companies to either license their training data or develop new methods for creating generative models that don't rely on copyrighted material. This could have significant implications for the future of AI-powered video editing and post-production.

  • AI video tools rely heavily on video training data
  • Potential need to license training data or develop new methods
  • Could reshape the AI video editing industry

Conclusion

The evidence suggests that AI video tools are facing a new and significant legal challenge over their use of copyrighted material in training datasets. While the full impact of these lawsuits is still unfolding, early rulings indicate a shift away from the permissive approach that has characterized AI development in recent years. Companies in the AI video space will need to adapt to this new legal reality, potentially by licensing training data or developing alternative approaches to model training.
