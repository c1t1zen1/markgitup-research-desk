State Courts Test Deepfake Evidence Rules as Fake Video Cases Surge

As deepfake evidence proliferates, state courts are developing divergent authentication standards while federal rulemakers debate a structured burden-shifting framework to address synthetic media in litigation.

Editor’s brief

Why this matters now

The legal system faces a growing challenge as artificial intelligence-generated content enters courtrooms, raising fundamental questions about evidence authentication. Multiple sources indicate that while federal rulemakers consider amendments to address AI-generated evidence, state courts are already grappling with these issues through existing evidentiary frameworks. The evidence gathered from legal scholarship, judicial proposals, and practitioner commentary reveals a field in transition, with significant disagreement about the optimal approach to authenticating synthetic media.

The analysis draws on proposals from the Federal Rules of Evidence Advisory Committee, academic commentary from law journals, practitioner guides from bar associations, and reports from judicial training organizations. Sources include the U.S. Courts website, legal practice groups, law school journals, and news reports on state legislative action. The evidence suggests a patchwork of approaches is emerging, with some states taking independent action while federal standards remain under development.

FIELD NOTE

Federal Rulemaking Debate: The Rule 901(c) Proposal

The Federal Rules of Evidence Advisory Committee has proposed an amendment to Rule 901(c) that would establish a burden-shifting procedure for addressing claims that audio or visual evidence has been generated or altered through artificial intelligence. Under this proposal, when a challenging party presents evidence suggesting material may be a deepfake, the burden would shift to the proponent to demonstrate the evidence's reliability. The mechanism is grounded in existing authenticity rules, requiring generative AI-manipulated evidence to meet authenticity and reliability standards before admission.

The proposal specifically targets what scholars call the "Liar's Dividend" - the phenomenon where parties make frivolous claims of fabrication to cast doubt on genuine evidence. By requiring proof establishing reliability when authenticity is challenged, the rule would prevent baseless deepfake allegations while still addressing legitimate concerns about synthetic media. The court would decide admissibility under Rule 104(a), the existing framework for preliminary evidentiary questions.

  • Rule 901(c) proposal creates burden-shifting when authenticity is challenged
  • Proponent must show probative value outweighs prejudicial effect
  • Targets frivolous deepfake claims while addressing genuine concerns
  • Court decides admissibility under existing Rule 104(a) framework

Alternative Approaches and Criticisms

Not all proposed solutions focus on Rule 901. The United States has released Rule 707 for public comment, which would address AI-created evidence. However, critics caution that Rule 707 applies only to evidence that the proponent acknowledges was created by AI, rather than evidence whose authenticity is genuinely in dispute. This limitation means Rule 707 would do little to help courts avoid admitting deepfakes when parties dispute whether material is authentic.

Some scholars advocate for expanding the trial judge's discretion in authenticating audiovisual evidence. One proposal suggests courts should be afforded flexibility to rely on multiple means to determine authenticity, including percipient witness authentication, digital forensics evidence, and expert testimony. This approach would amend the rules to involve novel authentication methods while maintaining the common-law theories applied to audiovisual evidence.

  • Rule 707 limited to acknowledged AI-created evidence
  • Critics argue Rule 707 doesn't address disputed authenticity
  • Alternative: expand judicial discretion for multiple authentication methods
  • Support for combining witness testimony with digital forensics

FIELD NOTE

State Courts Acting Independently

While federal rulemaking proceeds slowly - retired federal Judge Paul Grimm, who helped draft one of the proposed amendments, noted it takes a minimum of three years for a new federal evidence rule to be adopted - some states are acting independently. Louisiana's Act 250, passed earlier in 2025, requires attorneys to exercise "reasonable diligence" to determine whether evidence they submit has been generated by AI. This state-level action reflects growing judicial concern about the pace of federal reform.

Florida courts are also addressing AI-generated evidence through their existing evidence rules, requiring courts to assess authenticity, relevance, and admissibility of AI-generated images, audio, witness statements, or citations. The Florida Bar issued Ethics Opinion 24-1 addressing AI use, and Miami-Dade County implemented an AI disclosure order. These state actions create a patchwork of standards as different jurisdictions develop their own approaches to synthetic media evidence.

Practical Challenges for Judges and Practitioners

The AI Policy Consortium, a joint effort by the National Center for State Courts and the Thomson Reuters Institute, recently published bench cards as practical resources for judges facing evidentiary challenges from unacknowledged and acknowledged AI-generated evidence. These resources address manipulated evidence including deepfake videos, fabricated receipts, and manipulated photographs, which pose significant challenges for detection and authentication in court proceedings.

Legal practitioners face additional hurdles. Deepfake detection tools that output confidence scores but offer no audit trail may be challenged under Rule 702, which governs expert testimony. The authentication standard - requiring evidence to be what its proponent claims - is increasingly viewed as too low, especially given that authenticity is a threshold requirement for admissibility. Even scholars who previously criticized a generative AI-specific amendment to Rule 901 now advocate for change, recognizing that while very few cases so far have been impacted by deepfake allegations, the cases that do exist show staggeringly inconsistent results.

Closing read

Conclusion

The evidence indicates a legal system in transition, with multiple competing approaches to addressing synthetic media in courtrooms. Federal rulemakers are considering a structured burden-shifting framework through Rule 901(c), while state courts are developing independent standards through legislation and judicial interpretation. The pace of federal reform appears insufficient to address the immediate needs of courts already encountering AI-generated evidence.

What is clear from the sources is that the current authentication framework is inadequate for the AI era, though there is disagreement about the optimal solution. Some favor explicit federal rules addressing deepfakes, while others prefer expanded judicial discretion using existing evidentiary tools. The inconsistent results in existing cases suggest the need for greater standardization, whether through federal rulemaking or coordinated state action. The field is likely to see continued evolution as more cases involving synthetic media reach courts and as detection technology advances.

Five takeaways

  • Federal Rule of Evidence 901(c) proposal would shift burden to proponent when authenticity is challenged as potentially AI-generated
  • Rule 707, currently under public comment, applies only to acknowledged AI-created evidence and may not address disputed authenticity
  • Louisiana's Act 250 requires attorneys to exercise reasonable diligence in determining whether submitted evidence is AI-generated
  • Federal evidence rule adoption takes minimum three years, prompting independent state action
  • Existing cases involving deepfake allegations show staggeringly inconsistent results, suggesting need for standardized approach
