AI Influence on Congressional Committee Testimony and Expert Witness Selection

Examining how AI influences legislative input, this article explores the role of AI in curating expert testimony and shaping Congressional discussions.

November 24, 2026 · 7:13 PM PST · 4 min read · 12 sources · Original family: AI Lobbying & Policy in Washington

Editor’s brief

Why this matters now

This research examines the emerging intersection of Artificial Intelligence and the legislative process, specifically focusing on how AI technology may be utilized to synthesize, curate, or generate the input for expert testimony presented to Congressional committees. The evidence gathered points to active engagement between AI developers, industry representatives, and lawmakers who are seeking technical expertise to regulate the rapidly evolving field.

While the sources confirm the existence of AI technology and ongoing Congressional interest in AI regulation, direct evidence detailing the use of AI to *generate* expert testimony input is not present. Instead, the materials highlight the need for experts to advise Congress and the presence of AI-related testimony before committees.

Assignment angle: This angle focuses on how AI is being used to curate, synthesize, or even generate the input for expert testimony presented to legislative committees, moving beyond mere document drafting.

FIELD NOTE

The Landscape of AI in Policy and Testimony

Artificial intelligence is defined across multiple sources as technology enabling machines to simulate human learning, process information, and perform human-like tasks. Legislative bodies are clearly engaged with this technology, with plans for public AI hearings and calls for AI company CEOs to testify under oath before committees. Specific hearings have addressed topics such as the AI Security Landscape and the economic impact of AI on workers and employers.

  • AI is technology that enables computers to simulate human learning and perform tasks.
  • Congressional committees are planning public hearings regarding AI, including calls for CEO testimony.
  • Testimony has been submitted to committees addressing AI's economic impact and cybersecurity landscape.

Industry Engagement and Expert Input

Industry leaders and representatives are actively participating in the regulatory dialogue. Some entities, such as the Information Technology Industry Council (ITI), represent companies involved in AI development and adoption, providing testimony before Senate committees. Furthermore, there is a noted need for external tech experts to assist Congress in understanding the technology as it evolves.

  • Industry councils represent companies driving AI development and adoption in testimony.
  • Lawmakers are actively seeking tech experts to help them understand AI regulation.
  • Testimony has been submitted by experts covering areas like AI's economic impact.

The Potential for AI in Input Curation (Forecast)

While the current evidence shows AI being discussed *in* hearings, the potential for AI to be used in the preparation of testimony is implied by the technology's capabilities. AI assistants can aid in 'planning, brainstorming, and more,' suggesting a capacity for synthesizing complex information. It is possible that AI tools could be used by researchers or industry groups to rapidly synthesize vast amounts of data into coherent points for expert witnesses.

  • Generative AI tools are capable of assisting with brainstorming and planning.
  • The complexity of AI suggests a high potential for AI to be used in synthesizing policy inputs.
  • This remains a forecast based on the general capabilities of AI technology.

↗ Where it could go

  • AI can rapidly synthesize complex technical data, allowing experts to present comprehensive, well-researched testimony to committees.
  • AI tools can assist in identifying critical gaps in current regulatory frameworks by processing vast amounts of existing policy documents.
  • The availability of AI-driven insights could help lawmakers keep pace with the rapidly evolving nature of the technology.
  • AI can aid in structuring complex arguments, potentially leading to more nuanced and data-driven legislative discussions.

△ What could go wrong

  • Over-reliance on AI-generated input risks presenting synthesized information without the necessary human context or nuance required for legislative judgment.
  • If AI is used to curate testimony, there is a risk of algorithmic bias influencing which data points or expert perspectives are prioritized for lawmakers.
  • The complexity of AI means that even expert testimony may struggle to keep pace with the technology's advancements, as noted by the need for 'someone in the room.'
  • The industry's involvement, which some observers view critically, suggests potential conflicts of interest in the testimony being presented.

  • Testimony from AI developers detailing the internal processes used to generate their technical explanations.
  • Any formal guidance released by Congress on the acceptable use of AI in legislative research.
Closing read

Conclusion

The evidence confirms that AI is a subject of intense scrutiny within Washington, with hearings planned and expert testimony already provided across various domains, from cybersecurity to workforce impact. Lawmakers are actively seeking specialized knowledge to guide regulation.

Where the evidence is concrete is the *demand* for expertise and the *presence* of AI-related testimony. Where the evidence is thin is the direct documentation of AI systems being used to *generate* the core arguments or curated data points for those witnesses. The current material supports the idea that AI is a subject of policy discussion, not necessarily a tool for testimony creation, though the technology's capabilities suggest that line could blur.

Five takeaways

  • Congressional committees are actively scheduling public hearings to address AI, including calls for CEO testimony.
  • Industry groups, such as ITI, are providing formal written testimony on AI development to Senate committees.
  • There is an acknowledged need for external technical experts to help Congress understand the nuances of AI regulation.
  • AI technology is recognized as a system capable of simulating human learning and performing complex tasks.
  • Testimony has been submitted to House committees specifically addressing AI's potential economic impact on the workforce.
