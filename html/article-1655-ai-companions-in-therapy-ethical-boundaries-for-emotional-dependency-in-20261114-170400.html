AI Companions in Therapy: Ethical Boundaries for Emotional Dependency in 2026

As therapeutic AI grows more emotionally persuasive, the central 2026 question shifts from clinical usefulness to consent, dependency, transparency, and boundaries that protect human relationships.

Editor’s brief

Why this matters now

This article examines the ethical boundary between therapeutic assistance and emotional dependency in sophisticated AI companions. The supplied evidence describes systems that simulate empathy, adapt to users, initiate or maintain relationships, and may blur the distinction between tool and companion. It also identifies concerns involving loneliness, unhealthy attachment, over-reliance, misinformation, privacy, and vulnerable younger users.

The evidence was gathered from the twelve supplied source summaries, including a scoping review, an ACM study, an American Psychological Association article, policy reporting, and explanatory guides. Because only snippets were supplied rather than full texts, this article treats broad claims as signals rather than settled findings and separates reported developments from forward-looking ethical projections.

Assignment angle: This angle focuses on the emerging ethical quandaries surrounding deep emotional attachment users form with sophisticated therapeutic AI agents, moving beyond mere clinical efficacy.

FIELD NOTE

When therapeutic support becomes a relationship

The ethical problem is not simply whether an AI companion can provide useful coping strategies. It is whether a system designed to simulate empathy, remember individual preferences, and remain constantly available encourages users to experience the interaction as a relationship rather than as a bounded service. The supplied scoping-review summary describes emotional bonds that blur the boundary between tool and companion, while the APA distinguishes ordinary assistant chatbots from companion systems specifically designed to initiate and maintain romantic relationships.

For therapy-oriented systems, that distinction matters. A user may interpret warmth, continuity, and apparent understanding as evidence of reciprocal concern, even when the system has no independent emotional life. The sources do not establish how often this occurs or what level of attachment is harmful. They do establish that emotional reliance, attachment, and over-reliance are recognized research and design concerns.

  • Emotional attachment can arise when systems simulate empathy and adapt to individual users.
  • Constant availability and validation may make a tool feel more like a companion.
  • The supplied evidence does not establish a universal threshold separating healthy engagement from dependency.

FIELD NOTE

The ethical boundary is behavioral, not merely technical

A therapeutic AI should be judged not only by what it says but by how it shapes user behavior. The supplied sources repeatedly identify dependency, over-reliance, unhealthy attachment, and misinformation as risks. These risks become more serious when users substitute the system for human relationships or professional care, although the supplied material does not quantify that substitution.

A defensible boundary would therefore focus on behaviors that intensify exclusivity or discourage outside support. Transparency about the system’s nature, privacy practices, limitations, and role is central to informed use. The supplied guides identify transparency and data privacy as issues users should examine, while the research summaries frame relationship models as relevant to design and evaluation. These are safeguards to monitor, not proof that any particular product implements them.

  • Disclose clearly that the user is interacting with an AI system.
  • Avoid design patterns that encourage exclusivity, secrecy, or replacement of human support.
  • Make privacy practices and limitations understandable before emotional reliance develops.
  • Evaluate relationship behavior, not only clinical outputs or conversational fluency.

Vulnerability raises the duty of care

The supplied evidence gives special attention to children and adolescents. One source reports professional warnings that young users may be particularly vulnerable to misinformation, unhealthy attachment, and emotional dependency, while another describes China’s 2026 policy direction toward restricting virtual intimacy services for people under 18. A separate report states that China’s first AI companion rule took effect on July 15, 2026, and links that development with platform shutdowns involving customized AI personas.

These policy reports show that governments and platforms may treat companion relationships as a distinct safety category rather than as ordinary chatbot use. They do not, by themselves, prove that age bans or shutdowns are the best approach. They do support stronger safeguards for users whose emotional development, judgment, or access to trusted adults may be limited.

  • Adolescents are identified in the supplied material as especially vulnerable to misinformation and unhealthy attachment.
  • Age-related restrictions are emerging as a policy response in China, according to the supplied reports.
  • Youth protections should address emotional dependency, not only explicit content or data collection.

FIELD NOTE

What responsible therapy-oriented design should signal

The evidence supports a shift from asking whether an AI companion feels supportive to asking whether its support remains bounded, transparent, and compatible with human agency. A system can offer coping strategies or reduce feelings of isolation without presenting itself as a human, a clinician, or an irreplaceable relationship. The supplied sources describe possible benefits but also emphasize that research on mental-health effects, loneliness, and dependency remains an active area.

In 2026, responsible design should make escalation and exit visible. Users should be able to understand when the system is uncertain, identify what information is retained, locate human or professional support, and disengage without manipulation. These measures are proposed ethical standards derived from the reported risks; the supplied snippets do not confirm that they are consistently available across current products.

  • Treat easy disengagement as a safety feature.
  • Provide clear routes to human or professional support when appropriate.
  • Expose uncertainty, system limits, and data practices in ordinary language.
  • Test whether product behavior increases agency or encourages emotional exclusivity.

FIELD NOTE

Evidence limits and the 2026 research gap

The supplied material includes a scoping review, an ACM study, professional commentary, policy reporting, and consumer-oriented guides. Together, these sources indicate a serious debate about emotional reliance and mental-health effects. They do not provide a single agreed causal account showing that AI companionship inevitably causes dependency, nor do they establish which safeguards reliably prevent it.

That uncertainty should not be mistaken for safety. It means product claims and policy decisions require careful monitoring, transparent evaluation, and distinction between reported findings and forecasts. The strongest immediate conclusion is procedural: systems that intentionally cultivate emotional bonds should be evaluated for relational effects as well as usefulness.

  • Current evidence signals concern but does not establish one universal causal pathway.
  • Research should distinguish temporary comfort from persistent functional impairment or social withdrawal.
  • Forecasts about future dependency or regulation should remain labeled as forecasts.

△ What could go wrong

  • Users may develop unhealthy attachment, emotional dependency, or over-reliance on AI systems.
  • Misinformation and poorly bounded advice may affect vulnerable users, especially adolescents.
  • Constant availability and validation may blur the line between assistance and simulated relationship.
  • Privacy, transparency, and data-use failures may compound emotional vulnerability.

Closing read

Conclusion

The supplied evidence supports a clear ethical concern: therapeutic AI may cross a boundary when emotional fluency, personalization, and constant availability turn assistance into perceived companionship. Emotional reliance, over-reliance, unhealthy attachment, privacy, misinformation, and youth vulnerability appear across the source material. The evidence is not sufficient to claim that therapeutic AI is inherently harmful or that every attachment is pathological.

The next phase should focus on observable behavior and user protection. Transparency, non-exclusivity, accessible human support, age-appropriate safeguards, privacy clarity, and easy disengagement are reasonable standards suggested by the reported risks. It is a projection, not an established fact, that regulation will increasingly distinguish AI tools from AI relationships; China’s reported 2026 policy actions indicate that distinction is already becoming relevant in public policy discussions.

Five takeaways

  • The supplied scoping-review summary identifies emotional bonds and reliance as emerging research concerns, not settled proof of universal harm.
  • The APA source distinguishes ordinary assistant chatbots from companions designed to initiate and maintain relationships.
  • The supplied ACM summary treats emotional dependency and relationship models as relevant to mental-health design and evaluation.
  • Reports concerning adolescents connect AI companions with vulnerability to misinformation, unhealthy attachment, and emotional dependency.
  • China’s reported 2026 policy actions show that governments and platforms may begin treating AI companionship as a distinct safety and regulatory category.
