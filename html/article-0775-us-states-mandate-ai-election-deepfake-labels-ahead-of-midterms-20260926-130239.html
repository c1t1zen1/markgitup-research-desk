US States Mandate AI Election Deepfake Labels Ahead of Midterms

Thirty-three states have enacted laws addressing political deepfakes ahead of the 2026 midterms, creating a fragmented regulatory landscape with varying disclosure mandates and bans.

Editor’s brief

Why this matters now

As the 2026 midterm elections approach, individual US states have aggressively legislated against AI-generated political misinformation, with 33 states enacting specific laws by August 2026. This surge represents a significant shift from federal inaction, as states implement diverse strategies ranging from mandatory disclosure labels to outright bans on deepfake distribution during election windows. The evidence highlights a patchwork of regulations where obligations often fall on creators or distributors, leading to uneven protections for voters depending on their location.

The legislative push has largely been bipartisan, with every state passing such legislation citing bipartisan support. However, the implementation varies drastically; some states require year-round disclosures, while others impose temporary bans on deepfake distribution in the days leading up to an election. This decentralized approach faces immediate legal scrutiny, with some state laws already struck down on First Amendment grounds, signaling potential constitutional hurdles for remaining statutes.

Rapid Expansion of State-Level Legislation

The number of states with laws specifically addressing deepfakes in political communications has grown rapidly, reaching 33 by August 5, 2026, up from 28 at the start of the year. This expansion is tracked by organizations like Ballotpedia, indicating a swift legislative response to the proliferation of synthetic media. Public Citizen notes that these laws have received bipartisan support in every state where they have passed, suggesting a rare consensus on the need to regulate AI in elections.

Maryland recently joined the ranks as the 30th state to enact protections against political deepfakes, with Governor Wes Moore signing the new law. This momentum contrasts with federal efforts, where legislation has yet to reach a floor vote in either chamber. The state-level activity is driven by urgent concerns over voter manipulation, with legislatures moving faster than Congress to establish guardrails for synthetic media in campaign communications.

Divergent Regulatory Models: Disclosure vs. Bans

State laws do not follow a uniform model; instead, they employ varying mechanisms to combat misinformation. Some states, such as Minnesota and Texas, prohibit the distribution of deepfakes within a specific number of days before an election. In contrast, Maryland’s law imposes a year-round ban, while other states focus primarily on requiring disclosures when AI is used in political advertisements.

The scope of liability also differs significantly across jurisdictions. In some states, the obligation to disclose AI-generated content rests solely on the creator, while in others, it extends to any distributor of the ad. This means media companies and platforms could be held liable for distributing non-compliant ads, a provision that has raised concerns among broadcasters and legal analysts regarding the practical enforcement of these rules.

  • Minnesota and Texas prohibit deepfake distribution within a set number of days before elections.
  • Maryland enforces a year-round ban on political deepfakes.
  • Liability for non-compliance may extend to distributors and media companies in some states.

Legal Challenges and Constitutional Hurdles

Despite the legislative momentum, state deepfake laws face significant legal headwinds. According to the National Conference of State Legislatures, laws in California and Hawaii have already been struck down on First Amendment grounds. These rulings highlight the tension between regulating synthetic media and protecting free speech, particularly when laws restrict political speech or impose heavy penalties for distribution.

Other states are navigating similar risks. Massachusetts, for instance, has seen its election deepfake law stall in the state Senate, partly due to concerns about constitutional viability and the rapid pace of legal challenges elsewhere. As more states enact strict bans or broad disclosure requirements, the likelihood of further judicial intervention increases, potentially creating a complex legal map for campaign strategists and media organizations.

Conclusion

The landscape of AI regulation in US elections is currently defined by state-level action rather than federal policy. With 33 states enacting laws by August 2026, the majority of the electorate is covered by some form of deepfake regulation, though the nature of these protections varies widely from mandatory disclosures to temporary bans. The bipartisan nature of these legislative efforts underscores a shared concern about election integrity, yet the legal durability of these laws remains uncertain given recent First Amendment challenges in California and Hawaii.
