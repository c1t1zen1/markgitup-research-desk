Banks Now Use AI to Detect Fraud From Biometric Gait Analysis

Banks are moving beyond facial recognition and voice, exploring gait analysis from mobile sensors as a new biometric layer to catch AI-driven fraud in real time.

Editor’s brief

Why this matters now

Financial fraud is growing faster than traditional defenses can handle. AI-powered detection is now standard, but attackers are also using AI to forge identities, which is pushing banks to look for harder-to-spoof signals. Gait analysis — measuring how a person walks using smartphone accelerometers and gyroscopes — is emerging as one of those signals. This article examines the current state of AI fraud detection, why biometrics are shifting toward behavioral patterns like gait, and what the evidence suggests about the promise and limits of this approach. The analysis draws on industry guides, vendor research, and trend reports from 2024–2026, with particular attention to sources that discuss behavioral signals, false-positive reduction, and the evolving fraud landscape.

The Fraud Problem Is Growing Faster Than Rule-Based Defenses

Global financial fraud is estimated at $485 billion, and the pace is accelerating. Rule-based systems, which flag transactions based on static thresholds, are increasingly outmatched by coordinated, cross-channel fraud campaigns. Banks are turning to AI to process larger volumes of signals in real time, but detection alone is no longer enough — institutions need to reduce false positives and stop losses before they occur. The shift is from reactive alerts to proactive, continuous monitoring of customer behavior.

Why Biometrics Are Shifting From Static to Behavioral

Facial recognition and voice authentication have been widely adopted, but they are increasingly vulnerable to deepfakes and replay attacks. Behavioral biometrics — patterns that are harder to consciously replicate — are gaining attention. Gait analysis, which uses smartphone motion sensors to capture walking patterns, is one such signal. Unlike a photo or voice recording, gait is continuous and tied to physical movement, making it difficult to spoof without the person present. This aligns with the broader industry move toward real-time behavioral signals rather than point-in-time verification.

  • Static biometrics like facial recognition are vulnerable to deepfakes
  • Behavioral biometrics are harder to spoof and can be captured continuously
  • Gait analysis uses smartphone accelerometers and gyroscopes

How AI Fraud Detection Works in Practice

Modern AI fraud detection systems ingest multiple signals — transaction data, device fingerprints, location, and increasingly, behavioral biometrics. Machine learning models, including Graph Neural Networks, analyze these signals in real time to identify anomalies. The goal is not just to detect fraud after it happens but to predict and prevent it. Banks are also focusing on explainability, model governance, and regulatory fit to ensure these systems are reliable and auditable. The integration of gait analysis would add another layer of signal, potentially improving accuracy and reducing false positives.

  • AI systems use multiple signals for real-time anomaly detection
  • Graph Neural Networks are being deployed for complex fraud patterns
  • Explainability and model governance are critical for adoption

The Promise and Limits of Gait Analysis

Gait analysis offers a unique advantage: it can be captured passively through smartphones, which most customers already carry. This makes it a low-friction way to add a biometric layer without requiring new hardware or explicit user action. However, the evidence is still emerging. While the technology is promising, its real-world performance in banking environments — particularly in terms of accuracy, false-positive rates, and robustness across diverse populations — is not yet well documented. Banks will need to validate these systems rigorously before relying on them as a primary fraud detection tool.

  • Validation across diverse populations is needed

Closing read

Conclusion

The shift toward behavioral biometrics like gait analysis reflects a broader trend in AI fraud detection: moving from static, point-in-time verification to continuous, real-time monitoring. While the technology is promising, it is still in the early stages of adoption in banking. The evidence suggests that gait analysis could complement existing fraud detection systems, particularly in reducing false positives and catching sophisticated, AI-driven attacks. However, banks should approach it as one signal among many, not a silver bullet. Rigorous testing, transparent governance, and a focus on user experience will be critical to its success. As the fraud landscape continues to evolve, the integration of diverse, hard-to-spoof signals like gait will likely become more common, but the pace of adoption will depend on real-world performance and regulatory acceptance.
