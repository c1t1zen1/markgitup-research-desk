US States Race to Enact Deepfake Disclosure Laws Ahead of 2028

With federal deepfake legislation stalled, 49 states have enacted their own patchwork of disclosure and liability laws, creating a complex compliance landscape for media companies ahead of the 2028 election cycle.

Editor’s brief

Why this matters now

The regulatory environment for synthetic media in the United States has evolved from a handful of pilot laws into a sprawling, state-by-state mosaic. As of mid-2026, 49 states have passed at least one deepfake law since 2019, with 33 states carrying election-specific deepfake statutes into the 2026 midterms. This analysis examines the emerging compliance burden on media organizations, the legal challenges facing these laws, and the trajectory toward the 2028 election cycle. Evidence was gathered from legal trackers, law firm analyses, and regulatory databases documenting enacted statutes, court challenges, and pending legislation across all 50 states.

Assignment angle: While federal legislation stalls, a patchwork of state-level deepfake disclosure and liability laws is emerging, creating a compliance challenge for media companies.

FIELD NOTE

The Patchwork Problem: State-by-State Compliance

The absence of comprehensive federal deepfake legislation has resulted in a fragmented regulatory landscape that media companies must navigate. With 49 states having enacted at least one deepfake law since 2019, organizations operating across multiple jurisdictions face varying disclosure requirements, liability standards, and enforcement mechanisms. This patchwork creates particular challenges for national news organizations, political campaigns, and content platforms that distribute synthetic media across state lines.

The compliance burden extends beyond simple disclosure. Some states have enacted personality rights laws that address AI-generated content, while others focus specifically on election-related deepfakes. Washington State, for example, expanded its Personality Rights Law in March 2026 to explicitly cover AI-generated deepfakes, amending existing statutes to address "the growing use of artificial intelligence to create realistic but deceptive audio and video." Such laws impact not only content creators but also those who distribute or host synthetic media.

Disclosure Requirements and Watermarking Mandates

A significant portion of state deepfake legislation centers on disclosure requirements, though the specific obligations vary considerably. California's AB 853, known as the California AI Transparency Act (CAITA), represents one of the most comprehensive approaches, requiring generative AI providers to embed latent disclosures in AI-generated images, audio, and video. Effective August 2026, the law also mandates that providers offer manifest disclosure options, creating a dual-layer transparency framework.

However, not all states follow the disclosure model. While most states that have passed political deepfake laws include an exception for materials containing a disclosure statement, four states have enacted political deepfake prohibitions that do not include such an exception. This distinction matters significantly for media companies, as content that complies with disclosure requirements in one state may still violate prohibitions in another.

  • California's CAITA requires latent and manifest disclosures in AI-generated content
  • Most states exempt disclosed deepfakes from political deepfake prohibitions
  • Four states prohibit political deepfakes regardless of disclosure
  • Watermarking and metadata requirements vary by jurisdiction

Legal Challenges and Constitutional Questions

The rapid proliferation of state deepfake laws has already generated significant legal challenges, raising questions about the constitutionality of certain provisions. Hawaii's 2024 election deepfake law (Act 191, SB 2687) provides a cautionary example: enacted to address synthetic media in elections, the law was permanently enjoined on January 30, 2026, by a federal court in Babylon Bee v. Hawaii. The court found the statute to be an unconstitutional restriction on political speech, highlighting the delicate balance between regulating synthetic media and protecting First Amendment rights.

This litigation trend suggests that media companies may face not only compliance obligations but also uncertainty about which laws will ultimately survive judicial scrutiny. The injunction of Hawaii's law demonstrates that even well-intentioned deepfake regulations can be struck down if they are deemed overly broad or restrictive of speech. As more states enact similar legislation, additional constitutional challenges are likely, particularly regarding political speech and commercial expression.

Looking Ahead: The Road to 2028

As the United States approaches the 2028 presidential election, the regulatory landscape for synthetic media is expected to continue evolving. Legislative activity has shown no signs of slowing, with 204 deepfake-related bills tracked across 44 states in 2026 alone. The trajectory suggests that more states will enact disclosure requirements, expand liability provisions, and potentially target not just content creators but also the platforms and services that enable deepfake production and distribution.

Forecasts indicate that non-consensual deepfake bills may increasingly target generative AI platforms, payment processors, and hosting services that facilitate deepfake creation. This expansion of liability could fundamentally alter the business models of companies in the synthetic media ecosystem. Meanwhile, the absence of federal preemption means that the patchwork will likely persist, requiring media organizations to maintain sophisticated compliance programs capable of adapting to new state laws on an ongoing basis.

Closing read

Conclusion

The evidence clearly establishes that the United States has moved from an era of minimal deepfake regulation to one of extensive, though fragmented, state-level legislation. Forty-nine states have enacted at least one deepfake law, with significant variation in approach ranging from disclosure mandates to outright prohibitions. The legal challenges already emerging, exemplified by the injunction of Hawaii's election deepfake law, suggest that this landscape will remain fluid as courts grapple with the constitutional dimensions of synthetic media regulation.

What remains uncertain is whether this patchwork will eventually give way to federal legislation or whether states will continue to experiment with different regulatory approaches. The trajectory toward 2028 points to increasing complexity rather than simplification, with more states enacting laws and existing laws facing litigation. Media companies must therefore prepare for a regulatory environment that demands ongoing vigilance, state-by-state compliance analysis, and the flexibility to adapt as laws are enacted, challenged, and potentially struck down.
