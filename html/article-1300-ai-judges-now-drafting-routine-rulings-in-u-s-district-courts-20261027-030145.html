AI Judges Now Drafting Routine Rulings in U.S. District Courts

A Northwestern University study finds U.S. judges increasingly use AI to draft routine rulings and prepare for hearings, with a pilot program in L.A. County courts already underway.

October 27, 2026 · 3:01 AM PDT · 4 min read · 12 sources · Original family: AI in Legal Systems & Law Practice

Editor’s brief

Why this matters now

Federal and state judges across the U.S. are increasingly turning to AI tools to draft routine rulings and prepare for hearings, according to a Northwestern University study reported by The Daily Record and Spokesman-Review. This marks a shift from AI's earlier adoption in legal research and document review toward direct involvement in judicial decision-making. A pilot program in L.A. County courts has already deployed an AI tool called Learned Hand to help judges summarize motions and draft rulings in civil court cases.

Assignment angle: Focuses on the emerging use of AI to draft routine judicial opinions in U.S. federal courts, a development distinct from prior coverage of AI in legal research, citation checking, or document review.

FIELD NOTE

L.A. County Pilot Program

A select panel of L.A. County judges now have access to an AI tool called Learned Hand that can help them summarize motions and draft rulings in civil court. The pilot program, reported by the Los Angeles Times, represents one of the first formal deployments of AI for judicial opinion drafting in the U.S. court system.

  • Tool name: Learned Hand
  • Deployed to select panel of L.A. County judges
  • Focuses on civil court motions and rulings
  • Functions: motion summarization and ruling drafting
Source 12 · latimes.com
FIELD NOTE

Broader AI Adoption in Courts

The trend toward AI-assisted judicial work fits within broader adoption patterns. A Sedona Conference survey published in March 2026 found that over 60 percent of responding judges use at least one AI tool in their chambers, mostly for legal research and document review. However, nearly half of respondents reported receiving no AI training from their court administration, suggesting uneven preparation across the judiciary.

  • Over 60% of surveyed judges use at least one AI tool
  • Primary uses: legal research and document review
  • Nearly half of judges received no AI training from court administration
  • Survey published by Sedona Conference in March 2026

Judicial Response to AI-Assisted Filings

As AI use grows in legal practice, courts have been developing responses to AI-assisted filings. The American judiciary's approach has evolved from reactive crisis management toward more structured handling of AI-generated content in court proceedings. This includes orders issued, sanctions imposed, and disclosure rules adopted through June 2026.

Conclusion

The evidence indicates a clear trend: U.S. judges are increasingly using AI to draft routine rulings and prepare for hearings, with the Northwestern University study providing systematic documentation of this shift. The L.A. County pilot program with the Learned Hand tool represents a concrete implementation of this trend in civil court proceedings. This development extends beyond earlier AI adoption for legal research and document review, moving AI directly into the judicial decision-making process.
