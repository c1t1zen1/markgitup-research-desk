Why this matters now

The 2026 AI chip supply chain faces unprecedented pressure from rare-earth mineral shortages, driven primarily by China's expanding export controls. While the shortage narrative has evolved beyond GPUs to encompass the entire electronics supply chain, material constraints represent a distinct and growing bottleneck. Evidence gathered from industry analysis and supply chain reporting indicates that semiconductor supply chains, rather than production capacity, have become the critical constraint as AI demand surges.

The available evidence points to a systemic vulnerability in the global semiconductor ecosystem. TSMC's dominance in advanced chip manufacturing (controlling approximately 70% of capacity) combined with concentrated rare-earth supplies creates a fragile supply chain architecture. The evidence suggests that while companies have strategies for navigating these constraints, the situation remains fluid and potentially worsening as export controls expand.

The Rare-Earth Price Surge

China's export controls have triggered dramatic price increases in critical semiconductor materials. Industry reports document gallium prices rising 123%, germanium up 203%, and antimony increasing 144% in 2026. These minerals are essential components in semiconductor manufacturing, particularly for advanced AI chips that require high-performance materials.

The price spikes are not isolated incidents but reflect a broader pattern of supply chain disruption. China's strategic use of export controls as economic leverage has transformed what was once a stable commodity market into a volatile, geopolitically-driven pricing environment. The impact extends beyond raw material costs to affect production planning and investment decisions across the semiconductor industry.

Expanding Export Controls

China's rare-earth export controls have progressively widened throughout 2026, affecting an increasing number of firms and regions. In June 2026, controls were extended to 10 US firms, followed by expansion to 14 EU firms in July 2026. This pattern suggests a deliberate strategy to increase leverage over Western technology industries.

The expansion of controls indicates that the rare-earth shortage is not a temporary market fluctuation but a structural shift in global trade policy. Companies that previously operated outside the scope of these controls now face potential supply disruptions, creating uncertainty for long-term planning and investment.

Supply Chain vs. Production Capacity

A 2026 Moody's report identified semiconductor supply chains, rather than production capacity, as the critical bottleneck in AI chip manufacturing. This finding challenges conventional wisdom that focused on fabrication capacity as the primary constraint. The report highlighted that while TSMC controls 70% of advanced chip manufacturing, the real vulnerability lies in the upstream supply of materials and components.

This supply chain perspective has significant implications for understanding the rare-earth shortage's impact. Even with sufficient fabrication capacity, AI chip production cannot proceed without the necessary raw materials. The concentration of both manufacturing and material supplies creates a dual vulnerability that amplifies the impact of disruptions at either stage.

  • TSMC controls 70% of advanced chip manufacturing
