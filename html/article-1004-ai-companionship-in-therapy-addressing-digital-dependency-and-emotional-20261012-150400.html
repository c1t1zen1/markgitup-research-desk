AI Companionship in Therapy: Addressing Digital Dependency and Emotional Over-Reliance

Therapeutic AI may provide accessible support, but emotionally dependent relationships can blur care boundaries, reinforce isolation, and make healthy disengagement harder.

October 12, 2026 · 3:04 PM PDT · 6 min read · 12 sources · Original family: AI in Mental Health & Therapy
AI TherapyDigital EthicsMental HealthCompanionship
Editor’s brief

Why this matters now

AI mental-health tools and AI companions are related but not identical. The supplied evidence distinguishes assistant chatbots, which may be used as digital friends, from companion systems specifically designed to initiate and maintain romantic relationships. Another supplied guide groups CBT-oriented chatbots and companions together while emphasizing that research evidence and limitations must be examined separately.

The evidence base here is limited to the supplied source material: descriptions from professional, academic, institutional, and secondary sources. It supports cautious discussion of design risks, possible benefits, and reported associations, but it does not establish that therapeutic AI generally causes dependency or that every emotionally engaging system produces harm.

Assignment angle: This angle moves beyond simple diagnostic support to examine the psychological risks associated with deep emotional attachment to therapeutic AI agents.

FIELD NOTE

When support becomes a relationship

The central issue is not simply whether an AI can answer mental-health questions. It is whether the system encourages users to experience the interaction as a relationship with reciprocal emotional significance. The supplied APA material distinguishes ordinary assistant chatbots from companion systems designed to initiate and maintain romantic relationships, a distinction that matters when evaluating emotional attachment in therapeutic settings.

A therapy-oriented tool may be framed as structured support, while a companion may be optimized for continuity, personalization, or emotional closeness. The supplied evidence does not show that all such systems use the same design, but it supports treating relationship-building features as a separate risk category from diagnostic or informational assistance.

  • Emotional engagement is not equivalent to clinical effectiveness.
  • Systems designed to maintain relationships require different safeguards from general-purpose assistants.
  • The supplied evidence does not justify treating every AI mental-health tool as an AI companion.

FIELD NOTE

Digital dependency and emotional over-reliance

The strongest warning in the supplied material concerns vulnerable users with limited social networks. A Stanford summary reports that users seeking emotional support from AI chatbots experienced lower well-being, and another supplied source describes dependency as capable of shrinking a person’s real-world life. These descriptions indicate a reported association and a proposed failure mode, not proof that AI companionship harms every user.

Emotional over-reliance can also complicate disengagement. If a user increasingly turns to an AI for comfort, disclosure, or perceived companionship, the system may become a substitute for human contact rather than a bridge toward it. The supplied evidence does not quantify this process, identify a universal threshold for dependency, or establish whether effects differ across therapeutic designs.

  • Limited social networks are identified as a vulnerability context in the supplied Stanford summary.
  • Dependency may reduce engagement with real-world relationships rather than expand it.
  • Lower reported well-being should not be presented as proof of a universal causal effect.

FIELD NOTE

Designing for boundaries instead of attachment

The academic material supplied from ACM and arXiv proposes design implications centered on healthy boundaries, mindful engagement, disclosure without dependency, and surfacing relationship stages. These proposals are important because they treat dependency prevention as a product-design responsibility rather than solely a matter of user self-control.

Possible safeguards include making the system’s role legible, supporting pauses or limits, and avoiding interaction patterns that imply a human-like reciprocal relationship. The sources present these as design implications, not as validated requirements or evidence that existing products already implement them effectively.

  • Healthy-boundary features should be evaluated as core safety functions.
  • Mindful engagement and disclosure without dependency are explicit design goals in the supplied academic sources.
  • Relationship-stage cues could help users recognize increasing emotional investment, but their effectiveness remains unestablished.

FIELD NOTE

What the evidence can—and cannot—support

The supplied material points to a mixed research landscape. One guide describes CBT chatbots, AI companions, and research findings while explicitly framing the question around what randomized trials actually show and where tools fail. A separate supplied source refers to a scoping review that catalogued potential mental-health harms of large-language-model chatbots.

These references support continued scrutiny, not a simple verdict for or against therapeutic AI. Forecasts about future usefulness or future harm should remain labeled as forecasts. Stronger conclusions would require direct study designs, clear definitions of emotional dependence, comparisons with human support, and outcomes that distinguish short-term comfort from longer-term well-being.

  • Evaluate therapeutic claims separately from companionship claims.
  • Look for direct studies rather than relying only on summaries or promotional descriptions.
  • Separate immediate emotional relief from durable improvements in well-being and social connection.

↗ Where it could go

  • Structured CBT-oriented chatbots may provide an accessible form of mental-health support, according to the supplied guide’s description of the field.
  • AI systems could support disclosure while explicitly avoiding dependency, an intended design direction identified in the academic sources.
  • Boundary and mindful-engagement features could help users recognize and regulate increasing emotional investment.
  • Research comparing therapeutic tools with companion systems could improve evaluation of what genuinely helps users.

△ What could go wrong

  • AI companionship may worsen loneliness or lower well-being for vulnerable users with limited social networks, as reported in the supplied Stanford summary.
  • Emotional dependency may shrink a user’s real-world social life rather than strengthen it, as described in the supplied secondary source.
  • Systems designed to initiate and maintain romantic relationships may intensify attachment risks when their interaction patterns are transferred into therapeutic contexts.
  • Promotional or broad AI claims may obscure the difference between companionship, clinical support, and demonstrated treatment effectiveness.

◌ Watch next

  • Whether products clearly distinguish assistant, therapeutic, and companion functions, especially when systems initiate or maintain relationship-like interactions.
  • Whether independent studies measure social-network effects, well-being, and disengagement among users with limited social support.
  • Whether products implement and test healthy-boundary, mindful-engagement, disclosure, and relationship-stage features proposed in the academic sources.
  • Whether randomized trials and scoping reviews report durable outcomes and harms rather than only short-term user satisfaction or emotional relief.
Closing read

Conclusion

The supplied evidence supports a cautious thesis: AI companionship in therapy may create risks that are not captured by evaluating accuracy or diagnostic assistance alone. Relationship-oriented design, vulnerable users, and reduced real-world engagement are the relevant warning signals. The academic sources also indicate that healthier boundaries, mindful use, and disclosure without dependency are plausible design directions.

What is established here is narrower than a claim that therapeutic AI causes emotional dependence. The supplied sources describe distinctions, reported associations, design proposals, and research agendas. A reasonable projection is that systems deliberately optimized for sustained emotional connection will require stronger boundary and outcome evaluation than ordinary assistants, but that projection should not be treated as a settled empirical finding.

Five takeaways

  • The APA-supplied material distinguishes general assistant chatbots from companion systems designed to initiate and maintain romantic relationships [2].
  • The supplied Stanford summary reports lower well-being among users with limited social networks who seek emotional support from AI chatbots [8].
  • Academic sources identify healthy boundaries, mindful engagement, disclosure without dependency, and relationship-stage visibility as design goals [6,10].
  • A supplied guide frames CBT chatbots and AI companions as different parts of the mental-health AI landscape and asks what randomized trials actually demonstrate [4].
  • The supplied evidence supports caution and further study, but it does not establish that every therapeutic AI system causes emotional dependency or harm [4,6,8,10,12].
