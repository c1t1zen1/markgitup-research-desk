AI Video Models Face New Copyright Suits Over Synthetic Human Likenesses

Actors and influencers are increasingly suing AI video generators for unauthorized digital replicas, creating a legal battleground distinct from traditional training data copyright disputes.

FIELD NOTE

The Rise of Likeness and Voice Suits

A distinct legal front has opened where actors and influencers are pursuing AI video generators not just for copyright infringement, but for the unauthorized use of their identity. Unlike traditional copyright suits that focus on the reproduction of protected works, these cases target the creation of synthetic human likenesses and voices for commercial advantage without permission. This shift is evidenced by the emergence of liability for unauthorized use of voice and likeness, even in instances where copyright is not directly infringed. The risk is particularly acute when content includes synthetic voice, manipulated likeness, or realistic endorsements, creating a direct path to litigation for those whose identities are exploited.

Legal frameworks are adapting to treat digital damages similarly to physical injuries, emphasizing foreseeability and the duty of reasonable care. As noted in recent legal analyses, the use of a real person's identity for commercial advantage without permission creates a separate avenue for lawsuits. This development is supported by legislative efforts such as the Ensuring Likeness, Image and Voice Security Act in Tennessee, which aims to control voice identity in the era of generative AI. These measures indicate a move toward protecting personal identity as a distinct legal interest, separate from intellectual property rights.

  • Liability extends to unauthorized use of voice and likeness even without direct copyright infringement.
  • Commercial advantage through synthetic endorsements or realistic likenesses sharpens legal risks.
  • Legislative measures like Tennessee's ELVIS Act are emerging to protect voice identity.

FIELD NOTE

Copyright Limitations for AI-Only Output

While likeness rights are gaining traction, the copyright status of AI-generated video remains restrictive. Courts continue to uphold the human-authorship requirement, a stance reinforced by the Supreme Court's 2026 decision not to hear the appeal in Thaler v. Perlmutter. This decision confirms that purely AI-generated videos, lacking meaningful human creative contribution, generally do not qualify for copyright protection. Consequently, creators cannot rely on copyright law to protect their exclusive rights to purely synthetic outputs, leaving them vulnerable to unauthorized reproduction by others.

However, AI-assisted works that involve substantial human creative decisions, such as prompt design, curation, and editing, are more likely to qualify for protection. The boundaries of this protection remain legally untested, creating uncertainty for creators who use AI as a tool rather than a sole generator. The distinction is critical: if the final output captures the 'total concept and feel' of an original human-made image, infringement claims may still arise regardless of prompt changes, but the AI output itself may not be protectable if it lacks human authorship.

  • Supreme Court declined to revisit human-authorship requirement in Thaler v. Perlmutter (2026).
  • Purely AI-generated videos generally lack copyright protection.
  • Substantial human creative input in prompts and editing may qualify for protection.

FIELD NOTE

Distinguishing Training Data from Output Liability

It is crucial to distinguish between lawsuits targeting the training data of AI models and those targeting the specific outputs generated. Traditional copyright suits, such as those filed by copyright owners claiming unauthorized digital copies for training, focus on the reproduction rights of the underlying data. These cases argue that creating digital copies without permission to train AI systems infringes exclusive rights. However, the new wave of suits focuses on the output itself—specifically, the unauthorized replication of a person's likeness or voice.

This distinction matters because liability for unauthorized use of voice and likeness can exist even where copyright is not directly infringed. While studios like Disney are pursuing AI generators over copyrighted characters, individual actors and influencers are leveraging rights of publicity and new statutory protections. This creates a dual-track legal environment: one track addressing the legitimacy of training data, and another addressing the commercial exploitation of synthetic identities in generated content. The latter is becoming a more immediate risk for users and platforms deploying AI video tools.

  • Training data suits focus on reproduction rights; likeness suits focus on identity exploitation.
  • Studios are pursuing AI generators over copyrighted characters, while individuals pursue likeness rights.
  • Unauthorized use of voice/likeness creates liability independent of copyright infringement.

Closing read

Conclusion

The legal battleground for AI video generation is bifurcating into two distinct areas: the unresolved debate over training data copyright and the emerging, more immediate threat of likeness and voice rights violations. Evidence confirms that while the Supreme Court has solidified the human-authorship requirement, limiting copyright protection for purely AI-generated works, it has simultaneously left the door open for lawsuits based on the unauthorized commercial use of personal identities. This means that while a creator may not own the copyright to a purely AI-generated video, they can still be sued for creating a realistic digital replica of a real person without permission.

Looking forward, the risk profile for AI video users is shifting from abstract copyright concerns to concrete personal liability for identity theft and misappropriation. The passage of laws like Tennessee's ELVIS Act and the ongoing litigation trends suggest that courts and legislatures are prioritizing the protection of individual identity in the digital sphere. Creators and platforms must now navigate a complex landscape where avoiding copyright infringement is no longer sufficient to avoid legal action; respecting the rights of publicity has become a parallel and equally critical compliance requirement.

Five takeaways

  • The Supreme Court's 2026 denial of certiorari in Thaler v. Perlmutter firmly upholds the human-authorship requirement for copyright.
  • Unauthorized use of a person's voice or likeness for commercial advantage creates a separate legal liability from copyright infringement.
  • Purely AI-generated videos generally do not qualify for copyright protection, but AI-assisted works with human input may.
  • Legislative efforts, such as Tennessee's ELVIS Act, are specifically targeting the protection of voice and image identity.
  • Legal risks for AI video users now include both training data copyright issues and direct output liability for synthetic likenesses.
