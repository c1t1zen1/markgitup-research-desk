AI-Powered Jury Selection Tools Face First Federal Constitutional Challenge

A federal court will decide whether AI juror-profiling tools violate Sixth Amendment rights, testing algorithmic bias claims against constitutional guarantees.

FIELD NOTE

The First Federal Constitutional Challenge

A federal district court is set to rule on whether AI-driven juror profiling violates Sixth Amendment rights. This marks the first major legal test of algorithmic jury selection in U.S. courts. The challenge centers on whether AI tools that score, rank, and recommend which citizens to exclude from jury service undermine the constitutional guarantee of an impartial jury.

The case follows growing use of AI in jury selection and mounting scholarly concern. A law review article titled "Stacking the Deck: AI, Jury Selection, and The New Batson Problem" warns that these tools embed systemic bias, mask accountability, and erode constitutional protections. The article draws parallels to Batson v. Kentucky, which prohibited race-based peremptory strikes, suggesting AI may create a "new Batson problem" by encoding racial and gender biases into strike decisions at scale.

  • Scholars compare the issue to Batson v. Kentucky's prohibition on race-based strikes

FIELD NOTE

How AI Jury Selection Tools Work

AI jury selection tools scan a prospective juror's social media history, public records, and voting patterns within minutes of a name appearing on a jury list. These tools have evolved from passive data mining experiments into active profiling systems that score, rank, and recommend which citizens to exclude from jury service.

The tools promise objectivity by using data-driven analysis rather than attorney intuition. However, critics note that the algorithms may encode the same racial and gender biases that have historically affected jury selection, but at a larger scale and with greater opacity. The speed and breadth of data collection—analyzing social media, voting patterns, and public records in minutes—raises questions about the depth and accuracy of the profiling.

  • Tools scan social media, public records, and voting patterns within minutes

  • Evolved from passive data mining to active juror profiling

FIELD NOTE

Ethics Guidance and Professional Standards

The American Bar Association issued Formal Opinion 517 on July 9, 2025, providing ethics guidance on AI use in jury selection. The opinion tells lawyers they cannot follow an AI tool's recommendations without independent judgment, but it leaves the hardest questions unanswered about how to ensure fairness when algorithms may be biased.

The Los Angeles County Bar Association has also issued guidance, advising lawyers using AI jury selection programs to inquire whether the software's algorithm bases its evaluations on race, sex, religion, national origin, ethnicity, disability, age, sexual orientation, gender identity, marital status, or socioeconomic status. This guidance aims to help attorneys avoid using tools that may produce discriminatory results.

  • ABA Formal Opinion 517 (July 9, 2025) addresses AI use in jury selection
  • Opinion requires lawyers to exercise independent judgment over AI recommendations
  • LACBA guidance urges attorneys to investigate whether algorithms use protected characteristics

Research on Bias and Fairness

Closing read

Conclusion

The upcoming federal court ruling on AI-driven jury selection represents a pivotal moment for both legal technology and constitutional law. The evidence suggests that while these tools offer speed and data-driven analysis, they carry significant risks of encoding and amplifying historical biases. The ABA's ethics guidance and scholarly warnings indicate that the legal profession recognizes these concerns, but the guidance remains incomplete on how to ensure fairness in practice.

What is clear from the sources is that AI jury selection tools are already in use, scanning jurors' digital footprints and making recommendations to attorneys. What remains uncertain is whether the Sixth Amendment's guarantee of an impartial jury can accommodate algorithmic profiling, particularly if those algorithms reflect biased training data or opaque decision-making processes. The court's ruling will likely shape how these tools are used in American courts for years to come.
