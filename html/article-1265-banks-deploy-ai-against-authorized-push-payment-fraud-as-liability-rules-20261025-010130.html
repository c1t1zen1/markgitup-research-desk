Banks Deploy AI Against Authorized-Push-Payment Fraud as Liability Rules Tighten

Banks are adapting fraud controls for scams customers authorize themselves, as reimbursement expectations rise and social engineering outpaces traditional transaction monitoring.

October 25, 2026 · 1:01 AM PDT · 5 min read · 12 sources · Original family: AI Fraud Detection in Banking & Finance
authorized paymentsbanking fraudAI regulation
Editor’s brief

Why this matters now

Authorized push-payment fraud differs from unauthorized account takeover because victims are manipulated into approving transfers. The supplied evidence frames the issue around social engineering, real-time detection, bank collaboration, and changing liability expectations, rather than synthetic identities.

Evidence quality is uneven. Deloitte supplies a forecast of potentially nearly $15 billion in U.S. losses by 2028, while the Payments Association discusses broader structural and regulatory pressure. Chainalysis, IDnow, and the remaining sources provide contextual material, but the supplied snippets do not establish a complete legal framework or prove specific bank deployments.

Assignment angle: This angle examines how banks are adapting AI detection to socially engineered, customer-authorized transfers as regulatory liability and reimbursement expectations intensify, distinct from synthetic-identity fraud.

FIELD NOTE

The detection problem is behavioral, not merely transactional

APP fraud begins with a customer-authorized payment, often following social engineering. That makes conventional controls designed around stolen credentials or clearly unauthorized activity less decisive. Banks must assess whether a legitimate customer is being manipulated, pressured, or deceived at the moment a transfer is initiated.

The evidence supports a focus on real-time defenses and broader intelligence sharing, but it does not identify a specific technical model, bank system, or proven detection accuracy. Any claim that a particular AI approach is already standard would exceed the supplied material.

  • Customer authorization does not necessarily mean informed consent.
  • Social engineering shifts detection toward behavioral and contextual signals.
  • Real-time intervention is presented as important, but implementation details are not supplied.

FIELD NOTE

Liability pressure changes the economics of prevention

The supplied evidence describes a tightening liability environment. One source characterizes U.K. reimbursement for APP fraud as mandatory for participating banks and says liability is split between sending and receiving institutions. Another describes upcoming PSD3 and PSR as part of a broader shift in responsibility. These claims should be treated cautiously because the snippets do not provide primary regulatory text or full eligibility rules.

As reimbursement and consumer-protection expectations intensify, fraud prevention becomes more than a loss-avoidance exercise. Banks may have stronger incentives to detect suspicious payment journeys before funds leave, document intervention decisions, and coordinate with receiving institutions. The evidence supports this direction as an implication, not as proof that every bank has adopted these practices.

  • Reimbursement expectations can increase the cost of missed APP interventions.
  • Shared liability may encourage cooperation between sending and receiving banks.
  • Regulatory details and customer eligibility conditions require verification beyond the supplied snippets.

FIELD NOTE

AI is positioned as part of a collaborative defense

The Payments Association source describes a future shaped by AI-driven, cross-industry threats and calls for explainable AI and shared intelligence models. This suggests that banks may need systems capable not only of scoring individual payments, but also of incorporating network-level indicators and producing defensible reasons for friction, delay, or refusal.

That is a strategic direction rather than a demonstrated outcome in the evidence. The snippets do not establish that shared models are operational at scale, that explainability requirements are legally uniform, or that AI alone can reliably distinguish a coerced payment from an ordinary unusual transfer.

  • Explainability matters when interventions affect customers and reimbursement decisions.
  • Cross-bank intelligence may help identify mule accounts and recurring scam patterns.
  • AI should be treated as one layer in a control system, not a guaranteed solution.

FIELD NOTE

Scale of exposure remains a forecast, not a settled fact

Deloitte estimates that U.S. APP-fraud losses could approach $15 billion by 2028. This is a forecast, not a reported outcome, and the supplied evidence does not provide the assumptions, baseline, or methodology behind it. It nevertheless indicates why banks and policymakers may view APP fraud as a material strategic risk.

Other sources discuss 2026 rules and global reimbursement requirements, but the snippets do not establish a single worldwide standard. Readers should separate confirmed policy changes in a specific jurisdiction from projections about future loss growth or bank behavior.

  • The nearly $15 billion figure is explicitly an estimate for 2028.
  • Global APP reimbursement rules are presented in broad terms and need jurisdiction-specific validation.
  • Forecasted growth does not prove that AI controls will reduce losses.

↗ Where it could go

  • Earlier detection could reduce losses before authorized transfers become irreversible.
  • Explainable AI could help banks justify interventions and support reimbursement decisions.
  • Shared intelligence may improve identification of repeated scam patterns across institutions.
  • Greater regulatory pressure could accelerate investment in real-time payment defenses.

△ What could go wrong

  • False positives could delay legitimate customer payments or create harmful friction.
  • AI may misclassify socially engineered transactions when customer behavior appears normal.
  • Shared intelligence can create governance, privacy, and accountability challenges, although the supplied snippets do not detail them.
  • Forecasted loss growth may outpace banks' ability to deploy effective controls.

Closing read

Conclusion

The supplied evidence points to a clear operational challenge: banks must address payments that appear technically authorized but result from deception. Rising reimbursement expectations increase the value of earlier detection, stronger intervention records, and cooperation across the payment chain. AI, real-time analytics, explainability, and shared intelligence are presented as relevant responses.

The evidence does not prove that banks have solved APP fraud, that any named model is effective, or that liability rules are uniform across jurisdictions. Deloitte's projected nearly $15 billion in U.S. losses by 2028 is a forecast. The defensible conclusion is narrower: liability pressure is likely to push banks toward more context-aware and collaborative controls, while the results remain dependent on implementation, customer experience, and regulatory detail.

Five takeaways

  • APP fraud involves customers authorizing transfers after social engineering, making it distinct from purely unauthorized transactions (Sources 2, 5, 10).
  • The supplied evidence links changing reimbursement and liability expectations to greater pressure on banks and payment firms (Sources 3, 6, 8).
  • Explainable AI, real-time defenses, and shared intelligence are proposed responses, not proven universal deployments (Source 8).
  • Deloitte forecasts U.S. APP-fraud losses could approach $15 billion by 2028; this is a projection, not a confirmed loss total (Sources 10, 12).
  • The evidence supports jurisdiction-specific and implementation-focused analysis rather than a claim that one global APP-fraud rule or AI solution exists (Sources 3, 6, 8).
