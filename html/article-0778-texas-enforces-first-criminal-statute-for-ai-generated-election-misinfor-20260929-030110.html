Texas Enforces First Criminal Statute for AI-Generated Election Misinformation

Texas enforces criminal statutes against AI-generated election misinformation, marking a shift from disclosure mandates to prosecution for deceptive political content.

Editor’s brief

Why this matters now

Texas has established a rigorous legal framework for synthetic media in elections, transitioning from initial disclosure requirements to active criminal enforcement. Early legislation, specifically Election Code 255.004, pioneered the nation's first election-deepfake law in 2019, criminalizing the creation and distribution of deceptive videos within 30 days of an election. Recent developments indicate a move toward active prosecution, with high-profile accusations suggesting that state officials are prepared to leverage these statutes against political opponents. This evolution reflects a broader national trend where 28 states have enacted similar regulations, yet Texas remains distinct for its early adoption and potential for criminal liability rather than mere civil penalties. The evidence suggests that while federal laws like the TAKE IT DOWN Act address non-consensual imagery, Texas's focus on election integrity through criminal statute represents a unique and aggressive regulatory approach.

FIELD NOTE

From Disclosure to Criminal Liability

Texas was the first state to enact legislation specifically targeting the use of deepfakes in elections, establishing a precedent that has since been followed by numerous other states. The core of this legal framework is Election Code 255.004, which was enacted in 2019. Unlike many subsequent state laws that focus primarily on disclosure mandates—requiring labels on AI-generated content—Texas's statute includes criminal provisions. Specifically, it criminalizes the creation and distribution of deepfake videos intended to deceive voters and influence election results if distributed within 30 days of an election. This distinction is critical because it elevates the offense from a regulatory compliance issue to a potential criminal act, subjecting violators to prosecution rather than just fines or civil penalties. The law targets intent, requiring proof that the content was created with the specific purpose of injuring a candidate or influencing the election outcome.

Broader AI Governance and National Context

Texas's election-specific laws operate within a broader ecosystem of AI regulation, including the Texas Responsible Artificial Intelligence Governance Act. This act prohibits AI systems designed to manipulate human behavior to incite harm or criminal activity, adding another layer of potential liability for synthetic media creators. Nationally, the landscape is evolving rapidly, with 28 states having enacted laws regulating deepfakes in political communications as of January 2026. While many of these laws focus on disclosure requirements within a certain number of days before an election, Texas's approach remains distinct due to its criminal penalties. Federal efforts, such as the TAKE IT DOWN Act, primarily address non-consensual intimate imagery, leaving a gap in election-specific regulation that states like Texas are filling. The convergence of state-level criminal statutes and federal disclosure laws creates a complex compliance environment for political campaigns and media producers.

Closing read

Conclusion

The enforcement of Texas's criminal statutes against AI-generated election misinformation represents a significant milestone in synthetic media law. By moving beyond disclosure mandates to criminal liability, Texas has established a high-stakes regulatory environment for political deepfakes. The recent accusations against political figures suggest that these laws are no longer dormant but are becoming active tools in political disputes. However, the effectiveness of these laws depends on consistent enforcement and judicial clarity regarding the 'intent' standard. As more states adopt similar measures, the legal landscape for political advertising is becoming increasingly complex, requiring campaigns to navigate a patchwork of state-specific criminal and civil penalties. The Texas model may influence future federal legislation, particularly if state-level prosecutions demonstrate the feasibility and necessity of criminalizing deceptive AI content in elections.
