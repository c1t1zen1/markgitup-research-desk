Matter 1.5 Update: Interoperability Breaks for Legacy Smart Home Devices

Editor’s brief

Why this matters now

The Connectivity Standards Alliance (CSA) released Matter 1.5, an update focused on enhancing camera performance and expanding device flexibility for smart home ecosystems. Official documentation highlights the addition of support for cameras and video doorbells as a key milestone, aiming to streamline user experiences across different platforms. The update is described as incremental, designed to help device makers build higher-quality products while maintaining efficient operations for existing devices.

While the assignment angle suggests breaking changes for legacy Zigbee and Z-Wave bridges, the provided source material does not explicitly confirm widespread interoperability failures or specific technical breaks for these older protocols. Instead, sources indicate that some hubs, such as Homey, have received Matter 1.5 certification while remaining backwards compatible with earlier Matter versions. Third-party tools claim to assess compatibility, suggesting that some devices may require firmware updates or replacement, but specific details on bridge failures are not substantiated in the current evidence.

FIELD NOTE

Official Scope of Matter 1.5

The Connectivity Standards Alliance identifies Matter 1.5 as an incremental update that primarily enhances camera performance and expands device flexibility. This version introduces native support for cameras and video doorbells, marking a significant expansion of the protocol's capabilities beyond basic lighting and security controls. The stated goal is to enable device makers to produce higher-quality products and allow ecosystems to deliver more efficient and flexible user experiences.

The official narrative emphasizes improvement and expansion rather than disruption. By focusing on high-bandwidth devices like cameras, the update addresses a previous gap in the Matter standard. This suggests that the immediate technical focus is on integrating complex media streams into the Matter framework, which may have implications for network bandwidth and processing power but does not inherently imply the deprecation of older bridge technologies.

Hub Compatibility and Backwards Support

Evidence from specific hardware manufacturers contradicts the notion of universal breaking changes for legacy setups. For instance, Homey, a popular smart home hub, received Matter 1.5 certification for firmware version 13.3.0 in June 2026. Crucially, the manufacturer states that the hub remains backwards compatible with supported devices based on earlier Matter versions. This indicates that at least some major hubs can adopt the new standard without abandoning older connected devices.

This example suggests that interoperability issues may be device-specific or firmware-dependent rather than a systemic failure of the protocol for all legacy bridges. If major hubs maintain backwards compatibility, the 'breaking changes' angle may be more nuanced, potentially affecting only specific bridge implementations that do not receive updated firmware or certification.

  • Homey received Matter 1.5 certification in June 2026.

Closing read

Conclusion

The current evidence supports the view that Matter 1.5 is a significant feature update focused on cameras and doorbells, rather than a protocol overhaul that universally breaks legacy bridge compatibility. While the assignment premise suggests severe interoperability breaks for Zigbee and Z-Wave bridges, the provided sources highlight backwards compatibility in key hubs like Homey and describe the update as incremental. The narrative of widespread failure is not substantiated by the official CSA documentation or specific vendor statements in the provided text.

It is possible that specific, uncertified, or older bridge implementations face challenges, as suggested by the existence of compatibility checkers that flag devices for replacement. However, without direct evidence of a systemic protocol break, the situation appears to be one of gradual migration and firmware dependency rather than immediate obsolescence. Consumers should rely on specific vendor certifications and compatibility tools rather than assuming all legacy bridges are incompatible.
